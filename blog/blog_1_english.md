# How I Found My Way Into Research

I still remember the night it happened. I was a second-year student, sitting in front of my laptop at 2 a.m., running regression after regression on Eviews. The ARDL model kept returning a p-value above 0.1 for the long-run equation. Not statistically significant. I sighed, then started tweaking: dropped a few observations, changed the functional form, swapped out a variable. After hours of trial and error, the p-value finally landed at 0.04. I stared at that number for a moment.

Was this really what research was supposed to be?

---

I had no plan to become an economist when I entered Foreign Trade University. I barely had a concept of what economic research even meant. At first, I was just drawn to the basic courses — microeconomics, macroeconomics — the feeling of understanding why prices form the way they do, why markets sometimes fail.

The first thing that genuinely changed how I thought was a course called Principles of Economic Management, taught by Dr. Do Ngoc Kien in my second year. For the first time, I encountered the history of economic thought as a coherent story. The classical economists and the labor theory of value. Say's Law — the idea that supply creates its own demand. The marginalist revolution, which shifted economics from asking about growth to asking about efficient allocation. The diamond-water paradox: not because diamonds have higher intrinsic value, but because the marginal utility of your first diamond exceeds the marginal utility of your thousandth glass of water. Keynes, and the debate over whether free markets really deliver full employment in the long run. Dr. Kien did not just teach economics. He taught me how an economist sees the world.

I wanted to go deeper.

Like many students at my university, I took my first step into research by entering a student research competition. My topic was consumer behavior — the standard choice. I had no idea what I was doing. I stumbled through it, and I did not win anything. But what I took from that experience was a concrete problem: I did not know how to collect data, how to analyze it, or even what I was trying to prove.

So I threw myself into learning quantitative methods. ARIMA, GARCH, VAR, VECM, ARDL, then FEM, REM, GMM, SGMM. The more complex the name, the more I wanted to master it. I believed that good research meant using sophisticated methods. That complexity was what separated serious researchers from everyone else.

Then came that 2 a.m. night.

---

The question I found myself asking was simple: if research results change depending on what the researcher wants to find, what is research actually worth?

That was p-hacking. I knew it, clearly. I had changed the sample period, the functional form, added and removed variables — all to push the p-value below 0.05. I did not believe my own results. And yet I submitted them anyway. They were accepted.

I did not have a name for what I had done. I only knew something felt wrong. The ARDL method let me claim a long-run relationship, but I could not explain in economic terms why that relationship should exist. The SGMM gave me an impressive-looking table of results, but when someone asked what they meant economically, I could only recite memorized interpretations. I did not actually understand.

Was this really all research was?

---

The answer came from a friend. He told me: learn these methods and you will see a whole new dimension of research.

He was taking a course on impact evaluation, also taught by Dr. Kien. The methods he named were completely unfamiliar to me at the time — RCT, instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity.

I went home, opened YouTube, and found the Marginal Revolution University channel. That was the first time I heard about the Credibility Revolution in econometrics. The first time I encountered Esther Duflo and Joshua Angrist.

What stopped me in my tracks was not the complexity of these methods. It was how simple they were.

The core idea fits in a sentence: to identify the true effect of X on Y, you need a counterfactual scenario where everything else is held constant and only X changes. Then any difference in outcomes must come from X. No complicated formulas. No Hausman test, no Sargan test. Just a research design you can actually trust.

I realized I had been looking in the wrong direction for a long time. The sophistication in research does not come from complex tools. It comes from thinking carefully. From asking: how do I know this is causation and not just correlation?

I spent a long time after that immersed in causal inference.

---

But causal inference turned out to be only half the picture.

When I started interning at DEPOCEN, I met a researcher who had just completed his economics PhD in Japan. He was presenting his work on macroeconomic theory. I looked at the equations on the slides and understood almost nothing. But I was not put off. I was curious.

Because behind all those mathematical symbols, there were very concrete economic stories. A model lets you write down how households and firms behave, what they are trying to maximize, what constraints they face, and then derive the equilibrium. And that equilibrium often reveals things that empirical data cannot tell you directly. Empirics confirm that a relationship exists. Theory explains why.

The reading group at DEPOCEN made this clearer still. PhD students in economics would gather to work through papers by major researchers, presenting and arguing about the ideas. Sitting in those sessions, I started to understand: the best research rarely chooses between theory and empirics. It uses both. Theory builds the mechanism. Empirics measure the magnitude.

The question shifted from which method should I use to what is the economic mechanism here, and what empirical evidence can test it.

---

Looking back, I took a long detour to learn something that sounds obvious.

Research is not about proving you know a lot of methods. It is about asking an economic question that matters and answering it as honestly and rigorously as possible. Methods are tools that serve the question, not the other way around.

I am not embarrassed anymore about that 2 a.m. p-value I manufactured for myself. Because the moment I asked what am I doing right now, it changed how I think about research entirely.

And maybe that is something anyone entering academic work needs to experience at least once — not to find out where they went wrong, but to find their own answer to the question: what is research actually for?

I think I was lucky to learn this at 22. It gave me something to hold onto for the work ahead.
